Many of the installation calls we get in Burleson, TX are not replacements at all. A family adds a bathroom and needs more capacity. A homeowner wants the heater out of a hall closet and into the garage or basement. A remodel needs a second heater on the far side of the house, or a move to all-electric swaps a gas unit for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines that must be sized for the load, vents that must be routed and pitched correctly, circuits that must match the heater, and water lines that must be run cleanly. Burleson Water Heater works all of that out before the installation starts.

Sizing and placement shape most of the outcome. For a tank, the first-hour rating is the number we compare against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Then comes placement. A gas heater needs a vent route and combustion air, a heat pump heater needs open space and a condensate drain, and a whole-house electric tankless unit usually needs a big dedicated circuit and matching panel capacity. Any heater placed above a finished ceiling needs a drain pan, and a gas unit in a garage may need to sit on a raised platform where code requires it. We explain the trade-offs plainly and without pressure.

The final stretch is where a careful installer and a fast one part ways. Every gas joint is leak-tested, vent pitch and clearances follow the manufacturer's spec, the circuit and breaker match the heater, and dielectric fittings separate copper from steel. The relief valve discharge line runs full size to where it belongs, and expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps are installed when code requires them, with the permit handled when needed. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Catching a symptom early is often the difference between a quick part swap and a tank that gives out on the garage floor. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Big-tub or multi-shower demand the current heater cannot meet
  • Plans to change fuel types or go all-electric

  • Gallons wasted down the drain while you wait for hot water
  • Venting, relief valve piping, or gas connections that do not look right
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water
  • Morning showers that run cold for the last person

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

A new water heater installation is really several trades in one job. The gas piping must be sized for the heater plus every other appliance on the line, then pressure-tested until it is leak-free. The vent must meet the manufacturer's rules on diameter, length, pitch, and clearances, or carbon monoxide can find its way indoors. Electric and heat pump units need a circuit, breaker, and wire gauge matched to their load, and whole-house electric tankless units can draw more current than many panels have to spare. Then come the water-side details: shutoffs, dielectric transitions, a correctly rated relief valve with a full-size discharge line, and code items like expansion tanks, drain pans, and seismic straps that differ from place to place.

What you get from a pro is an installation that is done once. The gas line, vent, and circuit are planned before the first cut, the permit is handled when required, and the relief valve discharge and code items go in correctly. Every joint is tested, every gas install is checked for draft and carbon monoxide, and the temperature is set to protect against scalding. Hurried installs leave behind undersized gas lines, poor vent pitch, and missing expansion tanks. Ours are done to code and to last. And if the simpler option is the right one for your home, we say so plainly.

Water Heater Care Tips From Our Technicians

A little routine care goes a long way toward keeping a water heater running well past its expected lifespan. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Check the drain pan and relief valve discharge line now and then for signs of water
  • Set the thermostat near 120 degrees for safety and efficiency
  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
  • Nothing flammable belongs near a gas burner or its air supply
  • Drain a few gallons from a new tank twice a year to control sediment
  • Have the anode rod inspected every few years to protect the tank
  • Keep a clear path to the heater so it can be serviced and inspected easily
