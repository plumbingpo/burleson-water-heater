How do you know it is time to replace a water heater rather than repair it? Start with the age. Tanks tend to wear out between eight and twelve years, and the serial number on the label dates yours precisely. The symptoms come next. A leak from the tank itself, rusty water from the hot side only, and loud sediment noise all point to a tank near the end. A thermocouple, an element, or a relief valve can be swapped on almost any heater; a rusted-out tank cannot, and homeowners in Burleson, TX usually find that out the hard way, with a wet floor.

A new water heater should fit the way your household uses hot water today, not the way it did when the old one went in. That is why Burleson Water Heater starts with a few questions: how many people shower in the morning, whether you fill a large tub, and how many bathrooms draw at once. Tank heaters are compared by first-hour rating, while tankless units are sized by gallons per minute at the temperature rise needed in the coldest months. Heat pump models get a check for space and airflow, since they pull heat from the room around them. Gas line, vent, and panel capacity are all confirmed ahead of time, so installation day holds no surprises.

The swap itself follows a checklist, not a guess: the old tank drained and hauled away, new connectors and a working shutoff installed, a sediment trap added and the gas line leak-tested, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, and the relief valve piped to a safe discharge point. If local code requires a thermal expansion tank, a drain pan, or seismic straps, they go in, and the permit is pulled whenever one is needed. We finish by filling and purging the tank, checking draft and carbon monoxide on gas models, setting the thermostat near 120 degrees, and showing you around the new heater. Warning Signs to Watch For

Most tanks announce trouble weeks or months ahead with rumbling noises, rusty water, or hot showers that keep getting shorter. Keep an eye out for the following:

  • Visible corrosion around the pipe connections or burner area
  • A second or third repair within a year or two
  • Operating costs climbing on an older, less efficient heater
  • Rust-colored hot water while the cold stays clear
  • A tank at or past the eight-to-twelve-year mark
  • Loud rumbling or popping that flushing no longer fixes
  • Less and less hot water, even after repairs
  • Water seeping out from under the tank jacket

The Benefits of Professional Water Heater Replacement

Start with the venting. A gas water heater's exhaust contains carbon monoxide, and a vent that is undersized, poorly pitched, or loosely connected can let it spill back into your home. Add a gas connection that has to be leak-free, an electrical circuit that has to match the new heater's load, and a relief valve that has to carry the right rating with a discharge line that is never capped or undersized, and the details pile up quickly. Local code may also call for an expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps, and some areas want a permit. Switching to tankless or a heat pump adds new gas line sizing, vent materials, circuits, and condensate lines to the list.

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

A few preventive steps can help you avoid a cold shower or a wet floor down the road. Here are our recommendations:

  • Heat pump units need a clean filter and open airflow to run efficiently
  • Write the install date on the tank so you know when to plan the next replacement
  • Inspect the anode rod every few years; replacing it can add years to the tank
  • Make sure an expansion tank is in place if your home has a closed plumbing system
  • Avoid running the tank hotter than about 120 degrees unless there is a specific need
  • Descale a tankless unit every year, or more often with hard water
  • Start a flushing routine in the first year, before sediment hardens
