Keeping Your Arlington Water Heater Running Longer

Routine maintenance makes all the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that fails early. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: an annual sediment flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so problems are found while they're still inexpensive.

The age of your heater plays a big role. With regular service, a tank water heater usually runs 8 to 12 years, a heat pump water heater 10 to 15, and a tankless unit up to 20. Once a heater passes that range and starts acting up, like discolored hot water, rumbling, or a damp spot around the base, it's time to plan a replacement. Replacing on your own schedule is cheaper and is far less stressful than an emergency swap after a leak.

The sooner you call about a hot water problem, the smaller the repair tends to be. A sediment layer that's just starting to rumble needs only a flush, yet the same sediment, ignored through a few more years, can overheat the bottom of the tank and shorten the life of the tank, turning a simple visit into a replacement. Save our number in your contacts now so you can reach us fast as soon as something seems off. Early intervention saves time, money, and cold showers.

What Goes Wrong With Local Water Heaters

The majority of water heater failures we see in Arlington start with the water itself. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, insulating the water from the burner, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. Tankless units aren't immune either: scale builds inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Age is the other big factor. Plenty of homes around here still have a tank that was installed a decade or more ago, and once a tank is past the ten-year mark, the anode rod is usually spent and rust begins attacking the steel itself. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, gas tanks in some houses and electric tanks in others, so our technicians handle everything from worn thermocouples and sooty burners to burned-out elements and failed thermostats.

Homes on private wells may bring sediment, iron, or sulfur into the tank, and a rotten-egg odor in the hot water often means a reaction with the anode rod that a swap to a different anode type can solve. Older installations often need code updates when a heater is replaced: an expansion tank where a check valve or pressure regulator creates a closed system, a properly routed T&P discharge line, a drain pan where a leak could damage finished space, and earthquake straps where local code requires them. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most are much cheaper to address early.
