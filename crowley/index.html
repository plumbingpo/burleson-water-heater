How Crowley Homeowners Can Extend Water Heater Life

Routine maintenance is the difference between a water heater that runs for years and one that fails early. Our advice for homeowners is a short list: a yearly flush, an anode inspection every few years, a yearly test of the T&P valve, a thermostat set near 120 degrees, and annual descaling for tankless units on hard water, so small issues get caught early.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters run 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually last 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing warning signs such as rusty hot water, popping and rumbling, dampness under the tank, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is almost always cheaper and far less stressful than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

If warning signs do show up, acting quickly keeps them small. A dripping relief valve might point to excess pressure or thermal expansion with nowhere to go, both inexpensive fixes. The same drip ignored for months can wear out the valve and stress the tank until the tank itself fails, and a small repair becomes a full replacement plus water damage. It's smart to keep our number saved on your phone so you can reach us at the first sign of trouble. Early intervention saves time, money, and ruined floors.

Common Water Heater Problems in Crowley

Around Crowley, water heater trouble usually come from a handful of causes. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, hard water may be part of the picture: dissolved minerals settle to the bottom of a tank as sediment, forcing the burner or lower element to work harder, and that's what causes the rumbling and popping many homeowners hear during a heating cycle. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, which is why regular descaling is worth keeping up with where the water is hard.

Then there's age. Once the sacrificial anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel starts to rust from the inside, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and heating elements and thermostats on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater often means bringing the install up to current code: a thermal expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, plus straps and a pan where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the rotten-egg smell that occasionally develops when the water reacts with a standard anode rod. All of these is fixable, and most cost far less when caught early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
