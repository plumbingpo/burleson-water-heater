A tankless unit rethinks the whole idea of a water heater. There is no tank to keep hot, so there is no standby loss while you sleep or travel. When a faucet or shower opens, the unit senses flow and fires, heating water on demand through a heat exchanger. The result is a continuous supply for as long as you stay within the unit's rating, a compact footprint on the wall, and a lifespan that can be nearly double that of a typical tank. Families across Burleson, TX often choose tankless during a replacement or remodel for exactly those reasons.

Sizing is where tankless installs succeed or fail. We size by flow rate and temperature rise: add up the fixtures you realistically run at the same time in gallons per minute, then factor in how cold the incoming water gets in winter, since a unit that delivers five gallons per minute at a mild rise may deliver noticeably less when the groundwater is cold. Then comes the infrastructure. A whole-home gas unit can draw 150,000 to 199,000 BTU, which often means a larger gas line or confirming the meter can keep up. Venting must match the unit, and electric tankless models can need well over 100 amps, sometimes enough to require a panel upgrade.

We install every tankless unit with its future maintenance in mind. That means isolation valves for descaling, a gas line with a sediment trap sized for the burner's full demand, venting that meets the manufacturer's rules for length and termination, and a condensate line with a neutralizer on condensing models. If you have hard water, we explain scale protection options before the unit goes on the wall. Optional recirculation can shorten the wait at distant fixtures. Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • Showers turning lukewarm before everyone gets a turn
  • A home remodel or new build where the gas line and venting can be planned from scratch
  • Standby losses on a tank that sits idle for long stretches
  • A tank water heater that is ten years old or more and due for replacement
  • Wanting a unit that can last up to twice as long as a typical tank
  • A growing household or new bathroom adding hot water demand
  • A utility area where a bulky tank takes up room you would rather use
  • An addition or guest suite that is a long run from the main heater

What a Trained Technician Brings to the Job

Tankless installation is more involved than swapping a tank. A whole-home gas unit can demand several times the gas flow of a standard tank, and running it on an undersized line causes ignition faults and inconsistent temperatures. Venting is equally specific: condensing units use plastic vent pipe and need a condensate drain, non-condensing units need stainless steel, and neither can simply reuse the old tank's flue in most cases. Electric tankless can require multiple large circuits, and mistakes with gas, venting, or high-amperage wiring carry real fire and carbon monoxide risks.

Our job is to make tankless work the way it is supposed to. That starts with sizing for your busiest hour and coldest water, then making sure the home's gas, vent, and power can support it. We install with service valves for descaling, proper relief valve discharge, and venting built to the manual, then test under real demand before we leave. If a tankless unit is not the best choice for your home, whether because of cost, gas supply, or usage patterns, we say so and help you compare other options honestly.

Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

A few simple habits, like an annual flush and a quick look at the relief valve, can add years to a tank. Here are our recommendations:

  • Clean the inlet water filter screen a few times a year
  • Descale the heat exchanger once a year, or more often with hard water
  • Pay attention to error codes; they point to issues that are cheaper to fix early
  • Protect outdoor or garage units from freezing, and keep power on during cold snaps so freeze protection works
  • A setting near 120F limits scalding and reduces scale formation
  • Keep the air intake and vent termination clear of snow, leaves, and debris
  • Consider a water softener or scale-prevention filter if your water is hard
